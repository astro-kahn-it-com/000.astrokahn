Why Absolute Power Works In Society

## Chronological Chapter Breakdown

### 1. The Hobbesian State of Nature
The speaker introduces Thomas Hobbes' central argument by contrasting it with Robert Filmer's denial of the "state of nature." Hobbes posits that without authority, humans exist in a natural condition characterized by a "war of all against all," where life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."

> **"He says actually there is such a thing as a state of nature and it is a condition in which human beings are at war all against all."**

*Deep Analysis:* Hobbes' concept of the state of nature operates as a foundational theoretical construct to justify absolute rule. By framing the default human condition as one of violent chaos and perpetual insecurity, Hobbes establishes the necessity of a powerful corrective mechanism. The speaker points out that empirical evidence of this condition arises whenever law and order break down—such as in civil wars, natural disasters, or temporary anarchy. This perspective treats authority not merely as a divinely ordained given (as Filmer suggests) but as an essential, pragmatic imposition required for human flourishing, rendering the state a necessary defense against inherent human destructiveness.

### 2. The Necessity of an Undivided Sovereign
To escape the catastrophic state of nature, Hobbes argues that individuals must surrender their rights to a singular, undivided sovereign power. The speaker highlights Hobbes' critique of divided sovereignty—such as a balance of power between a king and parliament—asserting that such arrangements inevitably recreate the very conflicts the government was designed to prevent.

> **"Divided sovereignty, whether between king and parliament or across multiple bodies, recreates the very conditions of conflict and insecurity that the covenant was designed to escape. Unity of sovereign power is the prerequisite for peace."**

*Deep Analysis:* The core of Hobbesian absolutism lies in the belief that shared power is weak and unstable. By insisting on an undivided sovereign, Hobbes directly challenges the liberal ideal of checks and balances championed by later thinkers like Montesquieu and the American Founding Fathers. The speaker parallels this idea with Carl Schmitt's observation that power naturally tends toward convergence; true sovereignty belongs to the entity capable of making decisions during exceptional crises. In this framework, overlapping jurisdictions and competing branches of government only invite gridlock, systemic corruption, and eventual systemic collapse, underscoring the pragmatic value of a single, definitive decision-maker.

### 3. Monarchy vs. Parliament: The Alignment of Interests
The analysis transitions into Hobbes' structural arguments favoring monarchy over assembly rule. A crucial advantage of an absolute monarch is the alignment of the sovereign's personal interests with the long-term stability and prosperity of the realm, contrasting sharply with the fractured, short-term ambitions inherent in democratic or parliamentary bodies.

> **"In assembly rule, factional interests are baked in and multiply as a matter of course. Private ambition fragments decision-making as a matter of course."**

*Deep Analysis:* Hobbes perceptively identifies that a monarch views the state much as a patriarch views his household—as a lasting inheritance that must be preserved. Conversely, members of a parliament operate with short-term horizons, prioritizing re-election, factional loyalty, and personal advancement. The speaker vividly illustrates this by describing modern assemblies as breeding grounds for corruption, where each representative acts as a separate "node of corruption" easily influenced by special interests. By centralizing authority, a monarchy minimizes these competing private ambitions, ensuring clear accountability and a unified vision for the nation's welfare.

### 4. Law as the Sovereign's Command
The speaker discusses Hobbes' provocative definition of law, which explicitly rejects the idea that laws are discovered through unaided reason or derived from an objective "natural law." Instead, Hobbes asserts that law is simply the expression of the sovereign's authority; it is a command.

> **"Law is not determined or discovered by reason. It is commanded by the sovereign. It is a command... A law is the expression of the sovereign's authority, not a higher moral order."**

*Deep Analysis:* This represents a radical departure from classical jurisprudence. For Hobbes, justice is a meaningless concept outside the framework of a recognized authority capable of enforcing it. The validity of a law stems entirely from the sovereign's will and the power to execute it, meaning all law is fundamentally *de facto*. The speaker uses modern examples, such as selective enforcement of laws and the implementation of emergency decrees (like during COVID-19), to demonstrate that power dynamics ultimately dictate legal reality. This perspective strips away the moralizing veneer of the state, presenting law strictly as a tool of the sovereign to maintain order.

### 5. Freedom Under Absolutism
Addressing potential concerns about totalitarianism, the speaker presents a seemingly paradoxical argument drawn from Hobbes: citizens may actually experience greater personal freedom under an absolute monarch than under a legislative parliament.

> **"Because parliament is stacked full of lawmakers and this law and that law and legalism in general... thus you end up paradoxically with more freedom, personal liberty and freedom under the absolute monarch than you do under a parliament."**

*Deep Analysis:* Hobbes argues that the "purview of the king is actually quite small." Because an absolute monarch cannot micromanage every aspect of society, whatever the sovereign has not explicitly commanded remains an area of total freedom for the subject. In contrast, democratic parliaments are incentivized to constantly legislate, regulating increasingly minute details of civilian life and creating expansive legal bureaucracies. The speaker argues that the sheer volume of laws generated by assemblies encroaches upon liberty far more aggressively than the broad, generalized decrees of a singular monarch, challenging modern assumptions about the relationship between democracy and personal freedom.

### 6. The Subordination of the Church
Hobbes maintains that to prevent civil war, all institutions—including the church—must be subordinate to the sovereign. Allowing a religious body to claim an independent or higher authority creates a rival power structure that destabilizes the state.

> **"The church has to be subordinate to the sovereign. The sovereign must control public doctrine. Religious authority is firmly subordinated to the demands of civil peace."**

*Deep Analysis:* This point underscores Hobbes' prioritization of civil peace above all else. He recognizes that conflicting loyalties (e.g., to the state versus to the Pope or a distinct ecclesiastical hierarchy) are prime catalysts for violent conflict. By integrating spiritual and temporal power under one absolute ruler, Hobbes seeks to eliminate the ideological fractures that tear societies apart. The speaker acknowledges that while this may sound totalitarian to modern ears, it reflects a pragmatic response to the devastating religious and civil conflicts of Hobbes' era, illustrating a rigid adherence to the principle that "power cannot handle a rival caster."

## Key Quotes & Context

*   **"Without a common power to inspire all, the natural condition of mankind is a war of every man against every other man."** - This quote encapsulates Hobbes' justification for the state; humanity's inherent capacity for violence necessitates an artificial authority to impose peace.
*   **"The only thing that makes law real is enforced justice. Only an acknowledged authority capable of enforcement gives talk of justice any meaning."** - Highlights Hobbes' realist view that moral and legal concepts are irrelevant without the physical power to enforce them.
*   **"Good political order redirects and institutionalizes fear under a common power, transforming destructive terror into productive civil obedience."** - Demonstrates how the state channels natural, rational human fear into a stabilizing force that upholds societal order.

## Conclusion & Takeaways

Thomas Hobbes provides a rigorously logical, albeit controversial, argument for absolute monarchy based on a pessimistic view of human nature. By positing that the "state of nature" inevitably leads to destructive chaos, he justifies the necessity of an undivided sovereign capable of enforcing peace. His analysis critiques the structural flaws of divided governance, arguing that parliaments inherently breed corruption, factionalism, and excessive legalism, whereas a monarch possesses a unified, long-term interest in the realm's prosperity. Furthermore, by stripping law of its moral pretensions and defining it strictly as the sovereign's command, Hobbes forces a realist understanding of power. Ultimately, his philosophy suggests that surrendering certain rights to an absolute authority is not merely a submission to tyranny, but a pragmatic, rational trade-off required to secure the order and true liberty that allow civilization to function.
