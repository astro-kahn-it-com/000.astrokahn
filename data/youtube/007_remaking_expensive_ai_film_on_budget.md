### Chapter 3: Bringing the Scenes to Life: AI Generation Workflow

Moving from static images to moving video requires feeding the generated references into the video AI with precise context. The creator explicitly labels the reference images—main character, sidekick, crew members, enemy ship, cabin, and deck—so the AI understands the spatial and narrative relationships between them.

A critical aspect of this phase is translating the narrative script into technical prompts. To mimic the dynamic feel of a real film, the creator uses a speech-to-text tool (WhisperFlow) to quickly dictate complex camera movements and stylistic instructions to the AI agent.

> "And writing a script with the agent basically costs nothing. So, if you have a $100 budget, it's totally worth it to spend maybe two to five dollars on a good script... So, the cheat code to generating really nice-looking cinematic visuals is that I usually include these prompts for the agent. This makes the clip much more cinematic."

These prompts ("high energy, handheld," "snappy cuts," "film grain," "underexposed") instruct the video AI to behave like a physical camera operator in a chaotic environment, adding realism and grit to the scene.

To manage costs during the actual video generation, the creator limits generations to batches of three (as opposed to eight) and deliberately generates the raw footage at a low resolution of 480p.

> "Once the scene is described, I generate the clips in batches of three. Why three? Because we only have 100 bucks. I'm not going to run batches of eight. I also generate everything in 480p, and there is a very specific reason for that. [music] So, stay until the end. I'm going to show you exactly why I do it. By generating at 480p, the cost per second plummets. A 15-second generation batch costs barely over $3. This low-resolution output is then treated exactly like raw footage from a traditional film shoot. Instead of trying to generate a single perfect, continuous scene, the creator downloads the clips and brings them into editing software (CapCut).

> "At this point, I download all three clips and bring them into CapCut. [music] Then, I start splitting them into separate shots. Whenever a camera angle changes, I make a cut. I treat the generated clips like raw footage from an actual film shoot. From there, I can rearrange and select the strongest moments from each clip. This "Frankenstein style editing process" is the core of the creator's workflow. By editing as they generate, they can identify missing coverage (e.g., needing a close-up or a reaction shot) and generate only what is strictly necessary, rather than wasting money blindly generating variations of entire scenes. This hybrid approach—combining cheap, raw AI generation with traditional non-linear editing—is what allows the project to stay within the $100 budget while maintaining narrative coherence.
### Chapter 4: Refining Animations and Pushing AI Video Tools to the Limit

As the production moves into the second and third acts—a desert sequence and a jungle chase—the complexities of AI video generation become more apparent. The creator outlines a critical rule for generating complex interactions: do not prompt complex back-and-forth interactions within a single scene generation.

> "Another tip that I strongly recommend is to avoid prompting an entire back-and-forth sequence at once. Instead, approach it like a real film production, rather than asking the AI to constantly jump between our hero ship and the enemy ship. I generate one complete 15-second sequence showing everything happening on the hero ship. Then I generate another complete sequence showing what's happening on the enemy [music] ship. This keeps the prompt much simpler. You don't have to overwhelm the AI with multiple characters and location, which increases the risk of getting a bad generation."

By simplifying the prompts and isolating characters (e.g., separating the hero's actions from the bandits' actions), the AI is less likely to become confused and merge characters or environments. This technique drastically reduces the failure rate. However, isolating elements leads to a classic filmmaking challenge: maintaining screen direction.

In the desert scene, the AI generates the hero running to the right, but the chasing bandits running to the left. In cinematic language, this implies they are running toward each other rather than in a chase. The creator demonstrates that the solution is not to waste credits regenerating the clip until the AI randomly gets it right.

> "In this sequence, our hero runs toward the right side of the frame, but when we cut to the bandits, they run toward the left. This breaks spatial continuity and can make it look like they're traveling in opposite directions, or even moving toward each other. Now, there is a super easy solution to this. Just flip the clip... All you have to do is to mirror the footage, and it's done. So, now the spatial consistency is more clear."

This highlights a core philosophy of the video: use traditional, free post-production techniques (like mirroring a clip) to fix AI errors rather than relying on paid AI regeneration.

Similarly, in the jungle scene, the AI struggles to follow the prompt's direction for the character to run *away* from a threat, instead generating them running *toward* it. Rather than regenerating, the creator fixes the pacing and narrative flow by cutting the clip precisely on a music beat drop.

> "One challenge with C Dance is that it doesn't always follow screen [music] directions correctly. In this shot, I prompted the hero to run away from the monkeys, but he kept running toward them instead. Luckily, we can hide the problem with adding Over here, I found a music cue with a strong beat drop and cut from a close-up directly to the hero running... As a rule of thumb, always look for ways to improve your clips for free before regenerating them. You can trim your clips, mirror them, cut to music. You can do all of this without spending additional credits."

The creator does admit to one costly mistake: forgetting to include a reference image for a specific prop (a watch) in a close-up shot. The AI hallucinates a different watch, breaking continuity and forcing the creator to waste three generations to fix it. This reinforces the earlier point that meticulous pre-production and asset labeling are absolute necessities for budget-conscious AI filmmaking.

### Chapter 5: Post-Production, Upscaling, and Finalizing the Film

With all scenes generated and edited together, the creator tallies the final cost of the raw generation. The results are incredibly impressive.

> "In total, I generated 63 clips. All together, I used 9,150 credits, which comes to $64.05. Image generation added another $8.75, bringing the total cost to $72.80."

Generating a complete short film for under $75 is a massive victory, leaving room in the $100 budget. However, the film is currently in 480p resolution, which is unacceptable for a modern cinematic release. The final, crucial step is upscaling the footage to 4K.

This is where the creator's grand strategy regarding resolution is fully revealed. The cost to upscale the 63 clips using a dedicated AI upscaler (like Topaz Video AI or similar tools) is approximately $45. This brings the grand total to $117.80. While technically slightly over the arbitrary $100 budget, it is still a fractional rounding error compared to the $39,000 cost of the original Higgsfield film.

The creator explains the monumental savings achieved by this specific workflow:

> "Remember earlier when I told you to generate a video in 480p, and the reason to that is because we want to upscale it using an upscaler... Generating a 15-second clip in 4K costs 1,940 credits. That's around $13.58. I generated 63 clips in total. At 4K resolution, those clips would have required 1,200,000 credits. [music] That's equivalent to about $855. By generating in 480p and then upscale it to 4K, I probably saved around $791 [music] on this video."

This is the ultimate masterstroke of the workflow. Generating natively in 4K directly within the video AI platform is prohibitively expensive because it requires massive compute power to simultaneously synthesize the motion, the characters, and the high-resolution pixels. By separating the process—generating the *motion and narrative* cheaply at 480p, and then using a separate, specialized AI tool solely to *enhance the pixels* to 4K—the creator achieves the exact same visual fidelity for hundreds of dollars less.

## Conclusion & Takeaways

The experiment to recreate a $39,000 AI short film on a $100 budget is a resounding success, culminating at a final cost of approximately $117.80. This minor budget overrun is negligible when considering the staggering 99.7% cost reduction achieved compared to the original production. The project definitively proves that high-end AI filmmaking does not require unlimited financial resources, but rather a deep understanding of how to manipulate the tools efficiently.

The primary takeaway is that the "brute force" method of AI generation—endlessly typing prompts and regenerating until the AI produces a perfect clip—is a trap designed to drain subscription credits. True proficiency in AI video creation requires adopting the mindset of a traditional filmmaker. This means prioritizing rigorous pre-production, establishing strict visual constraints (like character sheets and specific camera simulations), and viewing AI generations as raw, imperfect footage that must be molded in post-production.

Furthermore, the workflow highlights the importance of separating generation from resolution. By generating scenes at 480p and utilizing a secondary AI upscaler for the 4K finish, creators can bypass the exorbitant costs associated with native high-resolution generation. Ultimately, this breakdown democratizes high-end AI production, providing a clear, actionable blueprint for independent creators to produce blockbuster-quality content without breaking the bank. The future of AI filmmaking belongs not to those who can afford the most credits, but to those who know how to edit, problem-solve, and engineer their prompts with surgical precision.
