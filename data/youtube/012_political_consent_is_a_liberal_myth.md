An examination of Robert Filmer's "Patriarcha," highlighting his empirical and historical challenge to liberal social contract theory, arguing that political authority intrinsically derives from natural, patriarchal hierarchies rather than mutual consent.

## 6-Word Premise

Political Consent Is A Liberal Myth

## Chronological Chapter Breakdown

### 1. Re-evaluating Filmer's Importance
The lecture begins by presenting Robert Filmer not merely as a historical footnote easily dismissed by John Locke, but as a foundational thinker whose arguments in *Patriarcha* require serious critical engagement. The speaker contends that Locke's refutations were primarily superficial, focusing on Filmer's biblical literalism rather than addressing the core of his political theory.

> **"...even though on the face of it it looks like Locke spent a long time refuting the arguments of Filmer, if you go back to the two treatises he actually spends most of his time not really dealing with the core argument and instead kind of making childish, sarcastic, kind of snide attacks at his Christian fundamentalism..."**

*Deep Analysis:* This establishes the framework for the entire video: a revisionist view of 17th-century political philosophy. By asserting that Locke sidestepped the philosophical heavy lifting by attacking the aesthetics of Filmer's biblical arguments, the speaker prepares the ground to decouple Filmer's core logic from religious dogma. This suggests that the foundational texts of liberalism (like Locke's *Two Treatises of Government*) might be built on shakier rhetorical grounds than traditionally taught in mainstream political science.

### 2. The Methodological Superiority of Filmer
A crucial comparison is drawn between Filmer's methodology and Locke's. The speaker asserts that Filmer built his arguments rationally from first principles and relied heavily on empirical observation of history, whereas Locke constructed theoretical abstractions divorced from reality.

> **"Secondly, Filmer goes back to first principles and he insists on the argumentation being rational... Second, what I'm going to argue is it is not actually essential to his argument. We can replace the Bible with Darwin, right?"**

*Deep Analysis:* The ironic contrast is powerful: Locke, famously known as the father of empiricism, is accused of relying on the unempirical fiction of the "state of nature" and the "social contract." Conversely, Filmer, often seen as a religious dogmatist, is championed for looking at how societies actually formed. The speaker's willingness to replace Filmer's biblical Adam with "Darwin" or evolutionary psychology underscores the argument that patriarchal authority is an observable historical and biological reality, not just a scriptural decree.

### 3. The Natural Origin of Authority
Filmer’s core thesis is elaborated: all human authority fundamentally stems from the family unit, specifically the authority of the father. Therefore, the state is not an artificial construct created by a contract, but an organic expansion of domestic rule into tribal and eventually national governance.

> **"And broadly speaking, as an empirical fact, most forms of authority in historic, you know, in the historical record have been patriarchal. It starts with the father as the protector and the governor of the family. And then political authority grows organically from the original domestic rule."**

*Deep Analysis:* This strikes at the heart of liberal democratic theory, which posits that humans are born free and equal and only submit to authority by choice. By arguing that authority is an organic outgrowth of the family structure—a structure nobody "consents" to be born into—Filmer (and the speaker) posit that hierarchy is the natural state of humanity. Attempting to base political legitimacy on mutual consent is therefore trying to build a society on an unnatural fiction.

### 4. Property Rights and Sovereign Power
The discussion shifts to the practical implications of sovereign power, particularly concerning property. The speaker explains that in Filmer’s view, all land and property rights are ultimately derived from the sovereign (the King), challenging the libertarian concept of absolute, pre-political property rights.

> **"...the authority to own that is derived from the king. And in the post-Lockean order, the authority is still derived from the government... It is now by the as Herbert Spencer would later say, the quote-unquote divine right of Parliament."**

*Deep Analysis:* This point demystifies the modern concept of private property. The speaker uses the modern reality of "eminent domain" to prove Filmer's underlying point: ultimate authority over land always resides with the state, regardless of whether that state is a monarchy or a liberal democracy. The "divine right of kings" hasn't disappeared; it has simply been transferred to parliaments or governments. This suggests that liberalism merely masked sovereign power rather than abolishing it.

### 5. The Destructive Nature of Consent Theory
The final major theme explores Filmer’s warning that basing government on the supposed consent of the governed (the social contract) inevitably leads to perpetual instability and revolution. If the people are the final arbiters of the contract, any government is always provisional and vulnerable to the shifting whims of the populace.

> **"Filmer regarded this as a recipe for permanent instability and civil conflict... Filmer argues that stable authority requires an origin beyond the popular will. One that is not susceptible to revision by each new generation."**

*Deep Analysis:* Filmer’s critique here is highly prescient regarding modern democratic struggles. The speaker links this to Carl Schmitt's concept of democracy disintegrating into warring interest groups. If authority must constantly be re-justified and consented to by every new generation (whose "values" constantly shift), long-term political and social cohesion becomes impossible. True stability, according to this view, requires an immovable bedrock of authority that transcends popular opinion and is rooted in natural law or historical precedent.

As an empirical fact, there was never a time at any moment in history where there was this you know, people just quote-unquote living in nature."** (Highlights the core disagreement with liberal foundational myths; human existence has always been governed by pre-existing structures of authority).

- **"His account confronts the fact of human dependence, natural hierarchy, and the necessity of clear authority more honestly than any contract theory."** (Summarizes the appeal of Filmer's theory—it deals with humans as they actually are, dependent and hierarchical, rather than as idealized autonomous agents).

## Conclusion & Takeaways

The exploration of Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha* serves as a profound critique of the foundational myths of classical liberalism. By examining human history and social evolution, the argument posits that patriarchal authority and natural hierarchy are empirical realities, whereas the "state of nature" and the "social contract" are intellectual fictions created by thinkers like John Locke. The enduring takeaway is that political power and authority are inevitable and organically derived, rather than conditionally granted by the consent of the governed. Relying on the illusion of consent not only misrepresents the true nature of power—which merely shifts from a monarch to a parliament—but also guarantees perpetual societal instability by making foundational authority endlessly debatable.
