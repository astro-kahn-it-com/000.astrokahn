## Chronological Chapter Breakdown

### 1. Introduction: The Leap from Cosmic to Microcosmic
The film opens by establishing the incredible breadth of subjects that animators must master, contrasting massive cosmic events (like the birth of a planet in *The Rite of Spring* from *Fantasia*) with the seemingly insignificant microscopic events of everyday life. This striking contrast serves to highlight the animators' core challenge: achieving believable motion regardless of the subject's scale or complexity. The narrator emphasizes that the difficulty of animation is not necessarily proportional to the size of the object being drawn. A massive planetary explosion might require grand, sweeping strokes, but the intricate physics of a tiny, delicate action demands an equally, if not more, rigorous level of scrutiny and precision. This opening chapter sets the stage for the documentary's central theme: the studio's unwavering dedication to studying reality to create convincing illusions. The "long, long jump" from the cosmic to the microcosmic underscores the versatility required of an effects animator, who must be as adept at rendering the catastrophic as they are at capturing the subtle.

### 2. The Anatomy of a Raindrop: Mastering Effects Animation
The documentary delves deeply into a specific, challenging example of effects animation: a single splash of a raindrop. The narrator explains that the natural speed of a falling raindrop makes it virtually impossible to study with the naked eye. To overcome this limitation, the animators turned to slow-motion photography, a recurring tool in their quest for realism. However, they discovered that water, being transparent, was not the ideal medium for revealing the complex mechanics of a splash.

In a brilliant example of practical problem-solving, the studio utilized milk instead of water. The weight, density, and opaque nature of milk made it the perfect substance to clearly reveal the intricate, rapidly changing shapes that transpire during a splash. The animators discovered that while all splashes share a basic sequence of events—the initial impact, the cratering, the upward rebound (the "crown"), and the secondary droplets—there is a surprising and beautiful variety in the specific pattern of each individual splash.

By meticulously analyzing these action patterns frame by frame, the animators were able to abstract and adapt these forms onto their drawing boards. They didn't simply copy the milk splashes; they interpreted them, capturing the essence of the fluid dynamics to bring greater authenticity to their drawn rain shapes. This rigorous process of observation and translation culminated in the creation of a believable "little raindrop," which, as the narrator notes, led naturally to the animation of an entire summer rainstorm. This section specifically references the "raindrop sequence" in the feature film *Bambi*, illustrating how the intense study of a micro-event directly contributed to the atmospheric realism of a major cinematic sequence. The segment emphasizes that effects animation is not merely about drawing water or fire, but about understanding the underlying physics and energy of those elements.

### 3. The Problem of the Flat Background
Having explored effects animation, the film shifts its focus to the evolution of the cartoon camera and the challenge of staging action. It begins by acknowledging that the "pre-feature" cartoon camera, which shot straight down onto flat layers of celluloid, was simple in construction and generally very satisfactory for short subjects.

A demonstration is shown using a classic Mickey Mouse short. Mickey is inked and painted on transparent celluloid sheets. To create the illusion that Mickey is walking through an environment, a panorama effect is used: Mickey walks in place (animated on a cycle), while a long, painted background is moved a fraction of an inch behind him for each frame. This frame-by-frame process is the bedrock of traditional animation.

However, the documentary points out the inherent limitations of this flat setup. While the animated character (Mickey) is drawn to have a real feeling of three dimensions—he can turn, approach the camera, recede, and possess an illusion of roundness—the environment he inhabits remains stubbornly flat. The narrator observes that when the character leaves the scene, "whatever dimension he has given the scene leaves with him."

The fundamental problem is that photographing a flat, two-dimensional painting fails to mimic how the human eye perceives a real piece of scenery. The documentary illustrates this with a moonlight scene. If the camera simply moves closer to a flat painting of a moonlit road, everything in the painting grows larger at the same rate, including the moon. In reality, as you walk down a road toward the moon, the objects close to you appear to change size and perspective rapidly, while the moon, being incredibly distant, remains fixed and appears the same size. The traditional camera setup created a false effect, a physical impossibility that undermined the desired realism of feature-length storytelling.

### 4. The Invention of the Multiplane Camera
To solve the problem of the unnatural flat background, the studio engineered a revolutionary piece of equipment: the multiplane camera. The documentary presents the blueprint for this "super cartoon camera," revealing a massive, towering structure designed to photograph different elements of a scene separated by varying distances from the camera lens.

The core concept of the multiplane camera is the separation of background elements. Instead of a single flat painting, a scene is broken down into multiple planes—foreground, midground, background, and distant elements (like the sky or the moon)—each painted on a separate sheet of glass. These glass planes are then stacked on different levels beneath the camera, which is positioned at the top of the apparatus, shooting downward.

The true magic of the multiplane camera lies in its ability to move these individual planes independently of one another. By calculating the correct relative speeds, the operators can move the foreground elements quickly past the lens, the midground elements more slowly, and keep the distant background (like the moon) relatively still. When the camera moves in on this multiplane setup, it accurately simulates the parallax effect of real-world vision. The foreground parts to reveal the midground, the perspective shifts naturally, and the moon remains the correct, fixed size.

The documentary returns to the moonlight scene, now shot with the multiplane camera, demonstrating how this technique successfully achieved a profound feeling of real depth and dimension. This technological leap was crucial for the studio's feature films, allowing them to create immersive, believable worlds that matched the sophistication of their character animation.

### 5. Operating the Multiplane Camera: A Collaborative Effort
The film provides a fascinating behind-the-scenes look at the multiplane camera crew in action, specifically preparing a complex shot for the opening scenes of *Bambi*. This sequence highlights the immense collaborative effort and technical precision required to operate this colossal machine.

We see the various levels of the camera, each holding its separate oil-painted glass pane. The camera operator sits high above, looking down through the lens to see all the separated levels combined into a single, cohesive picture. From this vantage point, the operator must orchestrate the independent movements of each plane.

The documentary shows the operators on the different levels adjusting their respective parts of the background, bringing them into the precise starting position for the scene. This process requires meticulous communication and coordination. We hear a brief exchange where the head operator, Dick, checks the setup with another operator, Alan, who is controlling the bottom level. Alan realizes he was moving his plane in the wrong direction and corrects it.

This interaction underscores the tedious, frame-by-frame nature of multiplane cinematography. Once everything is perfectly aligned, the exposure is taken, and the process must be repeated with slight adjustments for the next frame. The narrator reiterates a crucial point: when looking at a static frame of a multiplane setup, it might still look somewhat flat. The true illusion of depth is only revealed through *movement*—when the planes shift at their different, calculated speeds relative to the camera lens. The multiplane camera was not just a tool for taking pictures; it was a complex instrument for choreographing spatial relationships.

### 6. Caricature, Satire, and the Study of Ballet
Moving from technological innovation back to character animation, the documentary explores the vital role of caricature and satire in the animator's toolkit. To successfully caricature a subject—to exaggerate its prominent features for comedic or dramatic effect—an animator must first possess a thorough, foundational knowledge of that subject. You cannot effectively break the rules until you have mastered them.

In 1940, the studio decided to animate a satire of classical ballet for the *Dance of the Hours* sequence in the musical masterpiece *Fantasia*. The narrator notes that while ballet is a beautiful art form (referencing Edgar Degas's famous paintings), it was largely unexplored territory in the field of animation. The animators recognized that they lacked the specific knowledge of ballet mechanics required to caricature it convincingly.

True to their "school of self-improvement," the studio brought in professional dancers to teach the animators the fundamentals. The film shows live-action reference footage of a ballerina, Helene Stanley (who also modeled for Cinderella and Sleeping Beauty), demonstrating classical ballet steps. The animators are shown closely studying her movements, learning the vocabulary and mechanics of steps like the *pas de bourrée*, *arabesque*, *grand jeté assemblé*, and *développé*.

This intensive study was essential. By understanding the immense physical effort, the precise weight distribution, and the rigid formal rules of classical ballet, the animators could identify exactly which elements to exaggerate to create their satirical masterpiece. They needed to know how a real ballerina leaps in order to convincingly animate a hippopotamus attempting the same feat.

### 7. Designing the Dance of the Hours
The final major section of the documentary offers a delightful, staged look into a story meeting with key animators—Ollie Johnston, Frank Thomas, Marc Davis, and Milt Kahl (all members of Disney's famous "Nine Old Men"). This scene illustrates the collaborative brainstorming process used to adapt classical ballet into comedic animation.

The animators review the live-action footage of the ballerina and begin discussing how to translate those graceful movements to their distinctly ungraceful characters (ostriches, hippos, elephants, and alligators). They focus on specific physical details to emphasize the comedy. When watching the ballerina leap, they note the strength required for the takeoff and discuss how a heavy character (the hippo) would need to "push off with both legs" to move all that "beef."

The discussion turns to character design and the specific elements of caricature. They talk about exaggerating the hippo's sagging weight to "bring out the wrinkles" and create a "goofy little hop." They analyze the ostrich design, deciding that a long trunk (torso) that is out of proportion, combined with short, stubby, "hairy" legs and a "long scrawny neck," captures the perfect goofy look for their "prima ballerina."

A significant part of the conversation centers on the feet. One animator suggests giving the ostrich "big feet," comparing them to "size 12s" or "gunboats." They agree that emphasizing "funny little things that are typical of ballet," like the precise positioning of outsized feet, is where the real caricature lies.

The animators also discuss props and costuming. They consider a ballet skirt for the ostrich (which could humorously fall through on every action) and a dramatic, "sneaky" cape for the alligator villains in the final movement. This sequence brilliantly demonstrates that great character animation is not just about moving drawings, but about thoughtful design, observant caricature, and a deep understanding of the comedic potential of physical weight and proportion.

The documentary concludes by summarizing that out of these studio classrooms and collaborative sessions came the believable character animation, the realistic effects, and the improved camera techniques that defined the studio's golden age, culminating in the triumphant *Dance of the Hours* set to Ponchielli's music and conducted by Leopold Stokowski.
