This video explores the complex literary history and modern controversies surrounding author surrogates and self-inserts in storytelling, analyzing how they can either deeply enrich thematic resonance or catastrophically derail narrative integrity.

## 6-Word Premise

Self Inserts Can Enhance Or Ruin Stories

## Chronological Chapter Breakdown

### 1. The Audience's Response and the Catalyst for Exploration
The narrative begins by acknowledging a surprisingly overwhelming and highly engaged audience reaction to a previous analysis regarding "The Amazing Digital Circus," particularly the polarizing character of Jax. This impassioned discourse acts as the primary catalyst for the current exploration. The speaker notes that much of the vitriolic criticism surrounding Jax stemmed from a widespread perception that the character functioned as a "self-insert" for the creator, Gooseworx, allegedly serving as a vehicle to process personal gender struggles. The video observes a striking and consistent pattern: whenever the concept of a self-insert is introduced into critical discourse, audience reception plummets sharply. This widespread revulsion toward the idea of a writer injecting their own persona into their fictional world prompts a deeper, essential inquiry into the mechanics of storytelling. The speaker sets out to rigorously investigate the underlying causes of this phenomenon, asking whether contemporary writers must exercise greater conscientiousness regarding personal insertion, and fundamentally questioning if the very presence of a self-insert inherently degrades the structural and thematic integrity of a story.

> **"From the people who really did not like Jax, I noticed an interesting pattern in their criticism of how he was written. Most of those critiques and opinions centered on the idea that Jax was written as a self-insert for Gooseworx, using him to explore her own struggles with her gender. Anytime the self-insert idea was brought up, it was very clear that most people did not like it."**

*Deep Analysis:*
This opening segment brilliantly frames the central conflict by grounding theoretical literary criticism in immediate, contemporary pop-culture discourse. By highlighting the visceral audience backlash against the perceived self-insertion in "The Amazing Digital Circus," the video establishes that the concept is not merely an academic footnote, but a highly volatile element in modern media consumption. The analysis underscores the tension between an artist's desire for personal expression and an audience's expectation of narrative immersion. When viewers detect an authorial presence overriding the established fictional reality, the suspension of disbelief fractures. This chapter effectively posits that the "self-insert" label has mutated into a pejorative in popular critique, weaponized by audiences who feel alienated when a narrative seemingly pivots from universal themes to hyper-personal catharsis. The exploration is thereby necessitated not just as a stylistic review, but as a critical examination of the fragile psychological contract between the storyteller and the audience, setting the stage for a comprehensive historical and structural dissection of the trope.

### 2. Defining the Author Surrogate in Classic Literature
Moving beyond contemporary internet parlance, the video pivots to define the established literary terminology that predates the modern concept of the "self-insert." It introduces the term "author surrogate," framing it as a highly intentional, historically rooted writing technique utilized by legendary authors and analysts. An author surrogate is defined not merely as a self-portrait, but as a deliberate narrative mechanism wherein a character is constructed specifically to embody and articulate the author's personal perspectives, profound philosophies, and deeply held societal critiques within the confines of the story. To illustrate the historical efficacy and legitimacy of this technique, the video cites monumental works of fiction. Michael Crichton's deployment of Ian Malcolm in *Jurassic Park* is analyzed as a vehicle for Crichton's own intense anxieties regarding the ethical boundaries and hubris of genetic sciences. Similarly, the character of Benjamin the donkey in George Orwell's *Animal Farm* is presented as a manifestation of Orwell's own cynical, critical stance against totalitarian corruption. Through these classic examples, the video establishes that when executed with thematic precision, the author surrogate is a powerful tool for intellectual transmission.

> **"In literature, writers and literary analysts would use the term author surrogates to describe what is essentially the same thing. surrogate is explained as an intentional writing technique that transforms a character into the author themselves, allowing the author to share their own personal perspectives and philosophies within the story itself."**

*Deep Analysis:*
This chapter performs a crucial intellectual pivot, elevating the discussion from the often-dismissive realm of modern fandom critiques to the respected domain of classical literary analysis. By defining the "author surrogate," the video legitimizes the fundamental impulse of self-insertion, divorcing it from negative contemporary connotations of vanity or wish-fulfillment. The analysis of Ian Malcolm and Benjamin demonstrates that the most effective author surrogates are entirely subjugated to the thematic demands of the narrative. They do not exist to solve the plot effortlessly or to receive unearned adoration from other characters; rather, they serve as ideological anchors, articulating the core thematic anxieties that drove the author to construct the narrative in the first place. This section brilliantly highlights the paradox of a successful self-insert: it works best when it is least recognizable as a personal vanity project, and most integrated as an essential, philosophically cohesive component of the thematic machinery. The distinction drawn here is vital for understanding that the technique itself is neutral; it is the execution and the underlying intent that dictates its success or failure in resonating with a wide audience.

### 3. The Power of Thematic Purpose and Authorial Intent
Building upon the classical examples, this chapter delves deeply into the "why" behind the deployment of successful author surrogates. It asserts that the effectiveness of these literary devices is inextricably linked to the potent, overarching thematic messages of the narratives they inhabit. The video unpacks the central thematic question of *Jurassic Park*—"Can and will genetic science go too far?"—and illustrates how Michael Crichton's profound, personal conviction that scientific hubris will inevitably overstep humane boundaries necessitated the creation of Ian Malcolm. Because Crichton held this belief so intensely, an author surrogate became the most effective, authentic vehicle for expressing that specific dread. The same paradigm is applied to George Orwell's *Animal Farm*, where Orwell's core conviction that communism will inevitably be corrupted by the greedy required a narrative mouthpiece that represented his own unyielding worldview. The video concludes that these legendary storytellers did not utilize author surrogates for ego gratification, but as highly intentional, meticulously calibrated tools designed to amplify and clarify deeply personal warnings and philosophical convictions to their audience, ensuring the story's message resonates with maximum impact.

> **"The thematic question of the story is, 'Can and will genetic science go too far?' Michael Crichton believed that yes, genetic study can and probably will overstep its scientific and humane boundaries, and he wanted to illustrate that through his narrative. Thus, it makes perfect sense why he would put himself into the story. This is a message that he believes very strongly..."**

*Deep Analysis:*
This segment provides a profound psychological and structural analysis of the relationship between an author's core convictions and their narrative architecture. It posits that a successful author surrogate is essentially an ideological necessity rather than a self-indulgent luxury. The analysis highlights that when a story is constructed around a singular, burning thematic question, the author often requires an internal agent to advocate for the truth as they see it. This chapter effectively argues that the success of Crichton and Orwell lies in their ability to sublimate their egos into their thematic arguments. Their surrogates are not designed to be universally beloved by the audience or the other characters; they are designed to be right in the context of the story's philosophical debate. By establishing this high watermark of "intentionality," the video creates a rigorous standard against which all subsequent, less successful iterations of the self-insert can be judged. It underscores the critical difference between writing to explore a universal truth and writing to satisfy a personal desire, setting a theoretical baseline that is essential for understanding the structural failures discussed in later chapters.

### 4. Dante's Inferno and the Birth of the Ideal Author Surrogate
The narrative then transitions into an examination of what occurs when an author introduces a self-insert that flagrantly ignores established technical and thematic guidelines, utilizing the monumental example of Dante Alighieri's *Dante's Inferno*. While acknowledging the work as a profound exploration of theology and philosophy, the video highlights the almost comically transparent nature of Dante's self-insertion. As Dante journeys through the nine circles of Hell, his personal biases, vendettas, and literary admirations are glaringly obvious. The video highlights a specific, highly illustrative moment when Dante descends into Limbo and is greeted by legendary poets like Homer, Horace, and Ovid, who immediately welcome him as an equal, making him "one of their own band." This moment is pinpointed as a critical evolutionary step in literary history: the birth of the "ideal author surrogate." In this permutation, the self-insert ceases to function primarily as a vehicle for thematic exploration and instead transforms the narrative into an exercise in personal wish-fulfillment. The author uses the story to position themselves among their heroes, or to enact petty vengeance upon their political enemies, shifting the focus from universal meaning to deeply personal gratification.

> **"An ideal author surrogate is still the author inserting themselves into their own story, but now that self-insert transforms the story into more wish-fulfillment than thematic exploration. Instead of using an author surrogate as a way to express deep and personal beliefs and worldviews, an ideal author surrogate allows the author to go on their dream adventure with their favorite heroes."**

*Deep Analysis:*
This chapter is a masterful and slightly comedic deconstruction of one of Western literature's foundational texts, revealing it as the ultimate progenitor of the self-indulgent narrative trope. By analyzing *Dante's Inferno* through the lens of modern internet critique, the video bridges a massive historical gap, demonstrating that the human impulse toward narrative wish-fulfillment is timeless. The analysis of the "ideal author surrogate" is particularly sharp, as it identifies the exact mechanism by which a story degrades: when the narrative architecture is co-opted to serve the ego of the author rather than the integrity of the theme. Dante writing himself into a high-fiving circle with Homer and Ovid is hilariously exposed as an early form of authorial self-aggrandizement. This section is vital because it establishes that even legendary, canonical works are not immune to the structural pitfalls of the self-insert. It perfectly illustrates the shift from using a character to serve the story (thematic exploration) to using the story to serve the character (wish-fulfillment), a distinction that becomes the primary framework for analyzing modern iterations of the trope in subsequent chapters.

### 5. Fanfiction, A Trekkie's Tale, and the Evolution of the Mary Sue
Tracing the lineage of the "ideal author surrogate" forward, the video arrives at its most fertile modern breeding ground: fanfiction. This transition is described as unbelievably fitting, cheekily defining *Dante's Inferno* as "Bible fan fiction." The historical narrative zeros in on a pivotal moment in 1973, when Paula Smith published a short story titled *A Trekkie's Tale*. This satirical story featured a young lieutenant who flawlessly interacts with and leads the classic *Star Trek* characters. The character's name was Mary Sue, and in creating her, Smith unwittingly coined one of the most enduring and frequently cited character archetypes of the next half-century. The video delineates the Mary Sue as the next evolutionary stage of the ideal author surrogate. A Mary Sue is not merely a vehicle for wish-fulfillment; she represents an absolute extreme wherein the character possesses virtually no personal flaws, weaknesses, or meaningful struggles. The Mary Sue stands in a league of her own, warping the very fabric of the narrative universe around her perfection, and representing the ultimate culmination of self-insertion devoid of thematic restraint or narrative conflict.

> **"In 1973, Paula Smith published a short story called A Trekkie's Tale, a story about a young lieutenant who works on the starship Enterprise interacting and leading all of the classic Star Trek characters. This young lieutenant that Paula Smith wrote was named Mary Sue. And unbeknownst to Paula, she coined one of the most cited character archetypes that would live on for the next 50 years."**

*Deep Analysis:*
This section provides an essential historical etiology of the term "Mary Sue," tracing it from its origins as a specific satirical critique within a niche community to a ubiquitous cultural touchstone. The analysis highlights the crucial distinction between a standard ideal author surrogate and a true Mary Sue: the complete eradication of narrative friction. A Mary Sue does not just fulfill the author's wishes; she neutralizes the fundamental mechanics of storytelling, which rely on conflict, flaw, and growth. By examining *A Trekkie's Tale*, the video demonstrates how the archetype inherently destabilizes the established reality of a fictional universe. When a perfectly flawless self-insert enters a narrative, the surrounding characters are often reduced to mere props designed solely to validate and adore the surrogate. This chapter effectively solidifies the argument that the Mary Sue is a structurally destructive element, explaining precisely why audiences instinctively reject these characters. It represents the absolute prioritization of the author's ego over the audience's experience, providing a clear diagnostic tool for identifying when a self-insert has crossed the line from self-indulgent to narrative-breaking.

### 6. Mainstream Bleed-Over: From Twilight to Mindy Kaling's Velma
The narrative then examines the consequences of this fanfiction archetype bleeding into mainstream, commercially published storytelling. The video controversially but compellingly analyzes Stephanie Meyer's *Twilight*, arguing that it is structurally identical to the Mary Sue self-inserts found in fanfiction. Bella Swan is critiqued as a character who becomes the absolute center of attention across multiple factions, despite possessing virtually no defining character traits or strengths beyond her desire to be a vampire. The video then transitions to a more recent and contentious example: the 2023 animated series *Velma*. *Velma* is presented as a jarring blend of classic literary author surrogates and modern Mary Sue tendencies, created as a transparent self-insert to broadcast the creator's political worldview. The analysis posits that the creator understood the foundational concept of the author surrogate but calamitously merged it with the wish-fulfillment mechanics of an alternate-dimension fanfiction. The result was near-universal rejection, as the intense, heavy-handed political drive of the creator violently clashed with the established structure and identity of the *Scooby-Doo* universe.

> **"In 2023, Velma was released as a clear self-insert to express the creator's political worldview. And that did not go well. The creator of Velma understood the bones of why writers and authors would put themselves into a story, but also pulled on the fanfiction element with this alternate dimension version of Scooby-Doo. What resulted was a story that was universally hated..."**

*Deep Analysis:*
This chapter effectively synthesizes the historical and theoretical frameworks developed earlier in the video and applies them to modern, highly visible media failures. The inclusion of *Twilight* demonstrates that the Mary Sue structure can achieve massive commercial success while still being fundamentally hollow from a critical perspective, relying entirely on the audience's willingness to project themselves onto a blank-slate protagonist. However, the analysis of *Velma* provides the most potent illustration of the narrative collapse caused by a mishandled self-insert. The video astute diagnoses *Velma*'s failure not merely as a matter of unpopular politics, but as a severe structural dissonance. The creator attempted to utilize the profound thematic weight of an Orwellian author surrogate while simultaneously deploying the self-indulgent, universe-warping mechanics of a Mary Sue. This unholy amalgamation inevitably shattered the narrative integrity, as the story became hopelessly conflicted between delivering a serious message and catering to the creator's ego. This section serves as a definitive cautionary tale, proving that a self-insert that forces the narrative to bend to its will, rather than serving the narrative's themes, will inevitably alienate its audience.

### 7. The Mechanical Craft of Storytelling and the Proper Use of the Surrogate
In its concluding chapter, the video offers a prescriptive analysis for writers navigating the perilous waters of the self-insert. It asserts that the fraught history of the author surrogate definitively proves that storytelling is not merely an intuitive art form, but a rigorous mechanical craft requiring intense study and practice. The video warns that writers who fail to master this craft fall victim to the trap of writing solely for their own enjoyment, resulting in narratives that collapse under scrutiny. A clear distinction is drawn: while there is nothing wrong with writing wish-fulfillment fanfiction for personal entertainment, creating art designed to resonate with a broader audience demands a subjugation of the ego to the mechanics of narrative structure. The video concludes that an author surrogate can be a magnificent tool, but only when a storyteller understands how to deploy it to enhance the thematic resonance of the story, rather than using it to seek personal validation or to endlessly support their own biases and frustrations.

> **"If you want to tell stories that change people, if you want to tell stories that will mean something to someone and will allow them to look into your heart, mind, and soul as a storyteller, take the time to learn and to practice the craft. Storytelling is very artistic, yes, but it is also very methodical and mechanical in nature."**

*Deep Analysis:*
The final segment of the video is a powerful rallying cry for narrative discipline and structural integrity. It reframes the entire discussion, moving away from simple critique and toward a profound respect for the mechanics of storytelling. By emphasizing the "methodical and mechanical" nature of the craft, the video argues that the successful integration of deeply personal elements requires a mastery of objective narrative rules. The conclusion firmly establishes that an author surrogate is not an inherently flawed concept, but an incredibly potent tool that requires a highly skilled operator. When used to serve the story—to clarify a theme, to pose a difficult question, or to explore a complex philosophy—the surrogate elevates the art. When used to serve the author—to seek validation, to settle scores, or to avoid conflict—the surrogate destroys the narrative. This final analysis brilliantly synthesizes the entire essay, leaving the audience with a profound understanding that the line between a masterpiece and a Mary Sue is drawn entirely by the author's technical mastery and their willingness to prioritize the story above themselves.

## Conclusion & Takeaways

The exploration of author surrogates and self-inserts reveals a profound truth about the mechanics of narrative construction: the success of injecting oneself into a story is entirely dependent on authorial intent and technical execution. Historically, literary giants like Michael Crichton and George Orwell utilized author surrogates with surgical precision, subjugating their personal avatars to the overarching thematic arguments of their masterpieces. These characters functioned as essential ideological anchors, articulating the authors' deepest convictions regarding science and society. However, when this technique devolves into the "ideal author surrogate"—as seen historically in *Dante's Inferno* and contemporarily in the "Mary Sue" archetype of fanfiction and mainstream media—the narrative integrity collapses. Works that prioritize the author's wish-fulfillment, ego gratification, or unearned validation over organic conflict and thematic exploration inherently alienate their audiences, as vividly demonstrated by the disastrous reception of properties like *Velma*. Ultimately, while creating a Mary Sue is perfectly acceptable for personal amusement, crafting a narrative that truly resonates with a broad audience requires a rigorous mastery of storytelling mechanics. A writer must possess the discipline to utilize the self-insert as a tool to serve the story, rather than forcing the story to serve themselves.
