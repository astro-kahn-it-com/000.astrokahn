This video explores the cyclical nature of revolutions, drawing parallels between the French Revolution, the Russian Revolution, and other historical upheavals. It analyzes the periods of intense radicalism followed by reversals, often termed as Thermidorian reactions, where earlier revolutionary ideals give way to corruption, luxury, and eventual authoritarianism. The narrator discusses how societal decay, economic hardship, and moral permissiveness often precede the rise of tyrannical figures who restore order but solidify their own power.

## 6-Word Premise

Its always like this before revolution.

## Chronological Chapter Breakdown

### 1. The Thermidorian Reaction in France and Russia
The video draws striking comparisons between the aftermaths of revolutions in France and Russia. In France, the period following the Reign of Terror, known as the Thermidorian Reaction, saw a resurgence of high fashion, luxury, and moral permissiveness among the elite. Figures like Thérésia Cabarrus epitomized this new era of conspicuous consumption and murky wealth accumulation. Similarly, in Russia, the New Economic Policy (NEP) led to a revival of pre-revolutionary vices. Party elites indulged in decadent lifestyles, maintaining fleets of cars and embezzling state funds, while the lower classes continued to suffer.

> **"The merveilleuses, female counterparts of the jeunes dorés, began dressing in diaphanous gauze gowns in the middle of winter, which left very little to the imagination."**

*Deep Analysis:* The analysis highlights a critical phase in the revolutionary cycle where initial idealistic fervor gives way to cynicism and self-indulgence among the new ruling class. The rapid shift from revolutionary austerity to blatant luxury demonstrates the corrupting influence of power and wealth. The juxtaposition of the elites' lavish lifestyles with the dire poverty of the masses—such as inflation making basic necessities unaffordable in France—underscores the betrayal of the revolution's original promises. This phase of moral and economic decay creates a volatile environment ripe for further upheaval and the eventual rise of an authoritarian figure who promises stability and order amidst the chaos.

### 2. Economic Crisis and Social Inequality
As the new elites indulged themselves, both revolutionary France and Russia plunged deeper into economic crises. In France, massive inflation of the paper currency made it impossible for the poor to afford basic goods, leading to long lines for bread and widespread starvation. Desperation drove people to consume carrion and pets. The Russian provinces also saw widespread dysfunction, with local officials engaging in drunkenness, bribery, and abuse of power. The contrast between the luxurious lives of the few and the extreme suffering of the many created immense social tension and instability.

> **"In France, the inflation of the revolutionary paper currency made it impossible for the poor to afford the soaring prices. A bundle of firewood that had cost 20 livre in 1790 cost 500 livre in 1795."**

*Deep Analysis:* This section emphasizes the human cost of the post-revolutionary dysfunction. The stark economic disparities highlight the failure of the new regimes to deliver on their promises of equality and prosperity for all. The resulting desperation and starvation among the lower classes erode any remaining support for the revolutionary government. The widespread corruption and abuse of power by local officials further alienate the population. This period of intense hardship and lawlessness ultimately paves the way for a strongman leader who can restore order and provide basic necessities, even at the cost of political freedom.

### 3. The Rise of the Tyrant and the Cycle of Revolution
The chaos and lawlessness of the Thermidorian periods inevitably culminated in the rise of tyrannical leaders like Napoleon, Stalin, and Cromwell. These figures put an end to both the dysfunction of the reaction and the revolution itself. Even the American Revolution saw a milder version of this tendency, with the push for a strong national government under the Constitution to quell domestic disturbances and provide stability. The video argues that the cycle of revolution—from the decay of the old regime to moderate reform, radical violence, reversal, and finally tyranny—is a consistent historical pattern.

> **"The chaos, cynicism, and lawlessness of the Thermidorian period finally culminates in the emergence of a tyrannical leader who puts an end both to the dysfunctions of the Thermidorian reaction as well as the revolution itself."**

*Deep Analysis:* The analysis posits that the emergence of a dictator or strongman is a predictable outcome of the revolutionary cycle. The tyrant serves to restore order and stability, which the exhausted population desperately craves after years of upheaval and chaos. While the tyrant often rolls back many of the political freedoms gained during the revolution, they also consolidate power and enact reforms that permanently alter the social and political landscape. This suggests that revolutions, while cyclical, are not entirely futile, as they often result in long-term changes and the eventual remediation of the injustices that sparked them in the first place.

### 4. The Role of the Family and Sexual Morality
The video concludes by examining an often-overlooked aspect of revolutions: their connection to the family and sexual morality. It argues that revolutions frequently occur following periods of sexual permissiveness and conclude with the strengthening of marriage and traditional family structures. Figures like Napoleon, Stalin, and Augustus all enacted laws to reinforce marriage and restrict divorce or abortion. The analysis suggests a complex relationship between political instability, economic inequality, and sexual morality that warrants further study.

> **"Both Napoleon and Stalin, for example, strengthened marriage law and made divorce harder. Stalin outlawed abortion and in the 1930s... the Communist Party began propagandizing on behalf of the family."**

*Deep Analysis:* This intriguing perspective challenges conventional understanding by linking political upheaval to social and moral trends. The video argues that the breakdown of traditional family structures and sexual norms often precedes and accompanies the chaotic phases of revolution. Consequently, the restoration of order by authoritarian figures often involves the reimposition of traditional moral and social codes. This suggests a deep, albeit complex, connection between social stability, political order, and the institution of the family. This observation highlights the profound and far-reaching impact of revolutions, extending beyond mere political and economic structures into the very fabric of social and moral life.



### 5. Social Dynamics of Revolutionary Change
A deep dive into the social mechanics of revolutions reveals that they are rarely just political events; they are profound reorganizations of societal structures. When the old regime falls, the initial vacuum of power is often filled by idealistic moderates who genuinely attempt to address the grievances of the populace. However, this moderate phase is usually short-lived. As tensions rise and internal conflicts escalate, radical factions inevitably seize control, pushing the revolution to its extremes. This radicalization is fueled by the need to protect the revolution from both internal subversion and external threats, often leading to periods of intense violence and political purges, as seen during the Reign of Terror in France.

> **"At the beginning of the cycle the corruption and justice and dysfunction of the old regime grow until they become unbearable."**

*Deep Analysis:* The transition from moderation to radicalism is a critical juncture in any revolutionary timeline. The moderates, while well-intentioned, often lack the ruthlessness required to consolidate power in a chaotic environment. Radicals capitalize on the ensuing instability, presenting themselves as the true defenders of the revolution's ideals. This dynamic highlights a fundamental paradox: in order to save the revolution, the revolutionaries must often resort to the very tyrannical methods they initially opposed. The resulting violence not only alienates a significant portion of the populace but also sets a precedent that the ends justify the means. This moral compromise ultimately sows the seeds for the revolution's eventual downfall, as the populace grows weary of constant turmoil and begins to yearn for a return to normalcy and stability.

### 6. The Illusion of Immediate Transformation
Despite the dramatic upheaval associated with revolutions, the immediate post-revolutionary period often looks strikingly similar to the era that preceded it. The video emphasizes that revolutions rarely produce instantaneous, sweeping transformations in the distribution of social power. Instead, the newly established elites often adopt the mannerisms, luxuries, and corrupt practices of the very aristocracies they overthrew. This is not merely a failure of individual character, but a structural reality of power dynamics. When a new group assumes control of the state apparatus, they inherit not only its authority but also its inherent temptations and systemic flaws. The true, lasting changes brought about by revolutions typically unfold gradually over subsequent decades and centuries, slowly altering the societal fabric.

> **"And while it's generally true that revolutions do not immediately produce a decisive transformation in the distribution of social power... they come invariably as the crown of a flower whose seeds were planted during the revolution itself."**

*Deep Analysis:* This observation provides a crucial corrective to the romanticized view of revolutions as sudden, miraculous cures for societal ills. The immediate aftermath is often characterized by disappointment and disillusionment, as the lofty promises of equality and justice give way to the pragmatic realities of governance. The new leaders, faced with the monumental task of rebuilding a shattered nation, frequently prioritize stability and economic recovery over ideological purity. Consequently, the entrenched inequalities of the old regime often persist, albeit in new forms. However, the video astutely notes that the ideological seeds planted during the revolution—ideas of liberty, human rights, and popular sovereignty—continue to germinate, eventually flowering into substantial, long-term societal progress. This nuanced perspective encourages a more patient and realistic assessment of revolutionary outcomes.

### 7. Historical Precedents and the Inevitability of Reversal
The cyclical nature of revolutions is further evidenced by historical precedents beyond France and Russia. The video points to the American Revolution, suggesting that even this relatively stable transition of power experienced a milder version of the Thermidorian reaction. The initial period under the Articles of Confederation was marked by economic instability, localized rebellions (such as Shays' Rebellion), and a general sense of lawlessness. The subsequent push for a stronger centralized government under the Constitution can be seen as a necessary reversal, a reassertion of authority to prevent the nascent republic from descending into anarchy. This pattern underscores the inherent difficulty of maintaining a balance between liberty and order in the wake of political upheaval.

> **"People turned to Washington for safety with Madison, Hamilton, and the other Federalists, and against the opposition of radicals like Patrick Henry, he urged the formation of a strong national government..."**

*Deep Analysis:* By incorporating the American example, the analysis broadens the applicability of the revolutionary cycle. It demonstrates that the tension between radical democratic impulses and the need for centralized authority is a universal challenge faced by all post-revolutionary societies. The Federalists' success in establishing a robust national government highlights the pragmatic necessity of compromising some degree of local autonomy for the sake of national stability and economic viability. This historical comparison reinforces the central thesis that revolutions, by their very nature, generate counter-reactions. The initial burst of unrestrained liberty almost invariably necessitates a subsequent period of consolidation, where the raw energy of the revolution is channeled into enduring institutional structures.

### 8. The Psychological Toll of Perpetual Upheaval
Beyond the political and economic consequences, revolutions exact a profound psychological toll on the populace. The constant state of uncertainty, the pervasive fear of violence or persecution, and the rapid shifting of social norms create a deeply traumatized society. This collective trauma plays a significant role in facilitating the rise of authoritarian leaders. After years of chaos and deprivation, the populace becomes willing to trade political freedoms for the promise of security, stability, and a return to predictability. The tyrant, in this context, is not merely an opportunistic usurper, but a figure who fulfills a deep-seated psychological need within a fractured society.

> **"The chaos, cynicism, and lawlessness of the Thermidorian period finally culminates in the emergence of a tyrannical leader who puts an end both to the dysfunctions..."**

*Deep Analysis:* This psychological perspective is essential for understanding why populations so often embrace authoritarian rule in the aftermath of a revolution. The sheer exhaustion of living through constant turmoil erodes the individual's capacity for political engagement and critical thought. The primary concern shifts from abstract ideals of liberty to fundamental needs like safety, food, and social order. An authoritarian leader who can guarantee these basic necessities is frequently welcomed as a savior, rather than recognized as a threat to democratic principles. This dynamic highlights the fragility of democratic institutions in the face of sustained societal stress and underscores the importance of addressing the psychological dimensions of political transitions.

### 9. Bureaucratic Streamlining and Institutional Legacy
Despite the immediate failures and the eventual rise of authoritarianism, revolutions do leave behind a significant institutional legacy. One of the most common outcomes is the streamlining and modernization of the state bureaucracy. The old, inefficient, and often corrupt administrative structures of the ancien régime are swept away, replaced by more rational and centralized systems of governance. This bureaucratic modernization, while often implemented by autocratic leaders to consolidate their own power, ultimately strengthens the capacity of the state and lays the groundwork for future development.

> **"Government bureaucracy is streamlined and many cultural changes that occur during a revolution remain in place for a long time after."**

*Deep Analysis:* This point highlights the paradoxical nature of revolutionary outcomes. The autocratic regimes that typically emerge in the final phase of the cycle often accomplish the practical reforms that the initial revolutionary governments were too divided or incompetent to enact. By centralizing authority and rationalizing administration, these strongmen inadvertently build the infrastructure necessary for a modern state. This institutional legacy often outlives the tyrant themselves, providing a more robust foundation for future governance. This suggests that the true value of a revolution may not lie in its immediate political achievements, but in its ability to clear away the deadwood of the past, creating the structural preconditions for long-term societal advancement.

### 10. The Interplay Between Morality and State Power
The video's final assertion regarding the connection between revolutions and sexual morality merits further examination. It proposes that the breakdown of traditional moral codes is not merely a side effect of political upheaval, but an integral part of the revolutionary process. The initial phase of rebellion often involves a rejection of all established norms, including those governing family and sexuality. However, as the new regime seeks to consolidate its power and establish order, it inevitably recognizes the need for social cohesion. This leads to a subsequent period of moral retrenchment, where traditional family structures are aggressively reinforced by the state as a means of promoting stability.

> **"In almost every case revolutionary periods conclude not with the remediation of economic injustices... but when the period of sexual license that precedes it comes to a decisive end..."**

*Deep Analysis:* This analysis suggests a deeply intertwined relationship between personal morality and the power of the state. The revolutionary period of sexual license can be seen as an expression of absolute individual autonomy, challenging all forms of external authority. The subsequent authoritarian crackdown on these behaviors represents the state's reassertion of control over the private lives of its citizens. By regulating marriage, divorce, and reproduction, the state effectively harnesses the foundational unit of society—the family—as an instrument of social control. This dynamic highlights the extent to which political power relies on the regulation of personal behavior, and underscores the vulnerability of individual liberties in the face of state consolidation.



### 11. Cultural Resonance and Artistic Expressions of Upheaval
Beyond the political and institutional realms, the cyclical nature of revolutions profoundly impacts the cultural and artistic landscape of a society. The intense emotions, widespread suffering, and dramatic shifts in power generate a fertile ground for artistic expression. During the prodromal phase of a revolution, art often serves as a subversive tool, critiquing the decadence of the old regime and envisioning a utopian future. However, as the revolution descends into violence and subsequently into the Thermidorian reaction, artistic output frequently shifts to reflect the prevailing cynicism, disillusionment, and eventual return to traditionalism or state-sponsored propaganda.

> **"O Journal Universel commented that, 'Without anyone's realizing it, the vigorous style of the Republic is giving way to the surupy style of the monarchy.'"**

*Deep Analysis:* The cultural shifts during revolutionary periods provide a vital barometer for the societal mood. The transition from the 'vigorous style' of the Republic to the 'surupy style' of the monarchy in France exemplifies how art and culture mirror the political trajectory. The initial revolutionary zeal demands a stark, unyielding aesthetic that reflects its ideological purity. But as exhaustion sets in and the new elites begin to emulate the old aristocracy, the culture inevitably softens, embracing luxury and escapism. This cultural thermometer reveals that revolutions are not merely fought on battlefields and in parliaments; they are fought in the collective imagination of a people. The art produced during these eras serves as a poignant historical record of the hopes, fears, and ultimate compromises that define the revolutionary experience.

### 12. The Enduring Legacy of Revolutionary Ideals
While the immediate outcomes of revolutions often involve a return to authoritarianism and the reinforcement of traditional social structures, the ideals that sparked the initial upheaval rarely disappear entirely. The concepts of liberty, equality, and fraternity, once unleashed into the public consciousness, become incredibly difficult to permanently suppress. Even under subsequent tyrannical regimes, these ideals continue to circulate underground, inspiring future generations and providing a framework for ongoing resistance and reform.

> **"...when transformations of that type gradually come in the subsequent decades and centuries, they come invariably as the crown of a flower whose seeds were planted during the revolution itself."**

*Deep Analysis:* This final point is crucial for understanding the long-term historical significance of revolutions. A revolution may fail in its immediate objectives, and its leaders may succumb to the very corruption they sought to eradicate, but the ideological framework they introduce permanently alters the political landscape. The seeds of democratic thought, human rights, and social justice, once planted, possess an inherent resilience. They wait out the winter of authoritarianism, ready to blossom when the political climate eventually warms. This enduring legacy suggests that the true measure of a revolution's success is not found in the immediate aftermath, but in the slow, irreversible evolution of societal values over the longue durée.



## Conclusion & Takeaways

The cyclical nature of revolutions reveals a profound historical pattern where initial idealism frequently gives way to corruption, chaos, and ultimately, tyranny. This analysis underscores that revolutions are not simple, linear progressions toward liberty and equality, but complex phenomena fraught with predictable pitfalls. The Thermidorian reactions in France and Russia demonstrate how new elites often succumb to the very vices they sought to overthrow, leading to severe economic hardship and social inequality for the masses. This recurring dysfunction sets the stage for authoritarian leaders to seize power by promising the stability and order that the population desperately desires.

Furthermore, the video's exploration of the connection between political upheaval and sexual morality adds a unique dimension to our understanding of these historical events. The observation that revolutionary periods are often bookended by phases of sexual permissiveness and subsequent moral retrenchment suggests a deep interplay between social structures and political stability. Ultimately, while revolutions may seem to end where they began—under the rule of a strongman—they often catalyze long-term societal transformations. Recognizing these cyclical patterns is crucial for understanding the complexities of historical change and the enduring challenges of achieving lasting political and social justice.
