This video breaks down eight actionable techniques for authors to make their characters more memorable, engaging, and multi-dimensional, ensuring readers connect deeply with the story.

## 6-Word Premise
Readers Will Obsess Over Your Characters

## Chronological Chapter Breakdown

### 1. The Request Moment
A straightforward way to reveal character is by having one person ask another to do something they’d rather avoid. This interaction establishes friction, and the response uncovers the character’s true nature and values.

> **"A request moment isn't complex, it's just when one character asks another character to do something. And how that other character responds really tells us who they are as a person."**

*Deep Analysis:* The beauty of a request moment lies in its ability to strip away pretense. When forced to make a difficult choice—like the father in *The Road* deciding whether to help strangers—a character reveals their baseline compassion, pragmatism, or capacity for change, giving the reader immediate, actionable insight into their psychology.

### 2. Introduce Opposing Values
Giving a character conflicting core values creates intrinsic tension that drives compelling decision-making and character arcs.

> **"You don't want your characters to have two values that completely agree with each other. That doesn't get you anywhere. Instead, give them opposing values, values that are constantly tugging and in tension with each other."**

*Deep Analysis:* Internal conflict is the engine of a good character arc. When a character is torn between justice and mercy, or duty and desire, every decision carries immense narrative weight. This tug-of-war prevents characters from becoming flat or predictable, ensuring their journey remains dynamic.

### 3. Rapid Characterization Through Specific Markers
Use unique, highly specific markers—like a distinct prop, physical trait, or contradictory detail—to communicate a wealth of information about a character quickly, bypassing clichés.

> **"So, when you choose a better marker for your character, it delivers more information to the reader more quickly... It's also a great idea to use contradictory markers. For instance, someone has a facial tattoo, but then they're also dressed in a suit, and they're speaking with a very high diction level. That helps you to avoid stereotypes."**

*Deep Analysis:* Instead of relying on tired tropes like glasses for intelligence or leather jackets for rebellion, authors should seek markers that do heavy lifting. Contradictory markers are particularly effective because they immediately raise questions in the reader's mind, inviting them to learn more about the complexities beneath the surface.

### 4. Characterization Through Setting
Describe an intimate, personal space belonging to the character in a way that reveals their internal state, utilizing the environment to do the characterization work.

> **"The trick is to show one location that belongs to your character that reveals something about them that can't be revealed, I don't know, through dialogue or through things that they're doing in your story."**

*Deep Analysis:* A character's physical surroundings are an extension of their psyche. A cluttered, chaotic car or a sink full of dirty dishes communicates disorganization or depression far more effectively than exposition. This technique leverages subtext, allowing the reader to infer traits organically.

### 5. Cultivate Eccentricities
To avoid creating boring characters, authors should lean into oddities and quirks, particularly for protagonists, ensuring they stand out from everyday normalcy.

> **"Most of the time, it is a mistake to write perfectly normal characters. Why? Because we have lots of really boring people in real life... 95% of the time you want to work on making your characters extraordinary and interesting and different."**

*Deep Analysis:* While relatability is important, fiction demands characters who are memorable. Leaning into specific eccentricities—like hoarding jewels or outsourcing one's life to an assistant—creates a distinct silhouette for the character, making them vividly unforgettable without sacrificing their fundamental humanity.

### 6. Sensory Mapping
Isolate a single sense—like smell, taste, or sound—and map out the pivotal moments of a character's life exclusively through that lens to deepen their physical connection to the world.

> **"Choose a sense... And then I want you to choose one of your characters and try to map out their life solely based on that sense... It's a great way to sort of tap into who your character is and make them relate to the physicality of your world."**

*Deep Analysis:* This exercise forces authors to move beyond visual descriptions and engage with the visceral reality of their world. By defining a character's worst and best olfactory memories, for example, the author uncovers hidden traumas and desires, grounding the character in a deeply felt, tangible reality.

### 7. Meaningful Clothing Choices
Clothing should not merely reflect the historical period or setting; it must communicate specific, individualized information about the character’s personality, class, or internal struggles.

> **"What do your character's clothes say about them as a person?... One of the main mistakes I see when doing characterization through clothing is in fantasy or in historical novels, they make the clothing represent the period, but it's not actually saying anything about the character."**

*Deep Analysis:* Wardrobe is an active choice. A character's clothing can reveal their aspirations, their insecurities, or their sense of humor. By ensuring that attire serves a narrative purpose beyond mere world-building, authors add an accessible, immediate layer of characterization.

### 8. The Power of Contrast
A character's perceived goodness or villainy is heavily influenced by the moral alignment of the supporting cast surrounding them.

> **"Your character has a kind of barometer of goodness... That barometer is influenced by the people you surround them with. If you want your character to appear very nice, surround them with wicked people. If you want your character to appear really wicked, surround them with nice people."**

*Deep Analysis:* Context is everything. A flawed, degenerate protagonist can become tolerable—or even heroic—when contrasted against someone demonstrably worse. Conversely, a neutral character can seem abrasive when surrounded by extreme politeness. This relativity is a powerful tool for manipulating reader sympathy and shaping perception.

## Conclusion & Takeaways
Crafting compelling characters requires moving beyond basic physical descriptions and generic personality traits. By strategically employing techniques like request moments, internal value conflicts, sensory mapping, and environmental storytelling, authors can create multi-dimensional figures that resonate deeply. Furthermore, manipulating character perception through careful contrast and specific, meaningful markers ensures that these creations remain vivid, memorable, and obsession-worthy for the reader.
