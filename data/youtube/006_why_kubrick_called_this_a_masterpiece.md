## Executive Summary

Krzysztof Kieślowski’s *Dekalog* (Decalogue) stands as a monumental achievement in not just Polish cinema, but global filmmaking. Released at the tail end of the communist era in Poland, a period defined by martial law and the eventual triumph of the Solidarity movement, the series emerged as an intimate epic that captured the everyday struggles of ordinary people. By reimagining the Ten Commandments in a contemporary Warsaw tower block, Kieślowski, alongside co-writer Krzysztof Piesiewicz, managed to transcend overtly political critiques of the state, crafting a deeply philosophical and universal exploration of human morality, frailty, and empathy. Stanley Kubrick famously lauded the series for its unparalleled ability to dramatize complex ideas rather than merely discussing them, allowing the emotional and intellectual weight to hit the viewer profoundly and unexpectedly. The collaboration with Piesiewicz, a devout Catholic lawyer, and the financial backing from West German investors, afforded Kieślowski unprecedented creative freedom. This freedom allowed the director to assemble a rotating cast of cinematographers, ensuring each episode possessed a distinct visual palette while maintaining a cohesive thematic core. As a transition point between his earlier documentary-style social critiques and his later, internationally renowned French features (like the *Three Colors* trilogy), *Dekalog* remains the definitive capstone of Kieślowski's Polish career. It is a work that meticulously observes the "local" to illuminate the "universal," utilizing the confines of a concrete housing complex to reflect the vast, complex, and often tragic dimensions of the human condition.

## Chronological Chapter Breakdown

### The Twilight of Communist Poland and the Genesis of Dekalog

The historical context of *Dekalog* is inextricably linked to the socio-political climate of Poland in the 1980s. The decade was characterized by a profound sense of instability, beginning with the brutal imposition of martial law to suppress political opposition and culminating in the eventual collapse of the communist regime. Amidst this turmoil, Polish cinema was undergoing its own transformation, pushing boundaries and frequently clashing with state censors. Kieślowski, working at a remarkably prolific pace, sought a narrative framework that could speak to the pervasive economic and moral crises without resorting to overt political activism, which he increasingly found limiting.

> "The pinnacle of communist era Polish cinema came at its very end. The final decade of party rule was marked by both brutality and opportunity with the 1980s beginning with the imposition of martial law. The troops had moved in at midnight and ending with the victory of the Solidarity Union. Economic and moral crisis seemingly went hand in hand and the nation's post-war history was reconsidered aresh in daring films that often fell at the hands of the sensors."

In this fraught environment, Kieślowski developed *Dekalog*, a ten-part television series that would become his defining masterpiece. The series was deeply rooted in his concern for the ordinary citizens of the Polish People's Republic, yet it marked a significant stylistic and thematic evolution. Moving away from the direct social commentary of his 1970s work, *Dekalog* delved into profound philosophical inquiries, blending a documentary-like attention to everyday detail with an extraordinary degree of stylized visual beauty. This unique synthesis caught the attention of cinematic giants like Stanley Kubrick.

> "Stanley Kubri famously declared the decalogue a masterpiece writing that Kashlovski and co-writer Kashtov Pvich quote have the very rare ability to dramatize their ideas rather than just talking about them. They do this with such dazzling skill you never see the ideas coming and don't realize until later how profoundly they have reached your heart."

The collaboration with Krzysztof Piesiewicz, a lawyer who had previously consulted on Kieślowski's 1985 feature *No End*, was a crucial turning point. Piesiewicz, bringing his legal background and devout Catholic faith to the partnership, proposed the concept of adapting the Ten Commandments for modern times. This allegorical approach allowed the duo to bypass political themes while exploring profound moral dilemmas. Crucially, the project received funding from West German investors, which provided an essential layer of creative autonomy from Polish state broadcasters, enabling Kieślowski to fully realize his ambitious vision.

> "Determined to avoid overt political themes, the director and writer cast around for more allegorical material. It was the devout Catholic Perevich who landed upon the idea of a contemporary reimagining of the Ten Commandments. Crucially, while the Decalogue series was made for Polish television, it was funded by West German investors, which granted Pierce Chevich and Koshlovski a degree of authorship and creative agency rarely afforded by state broadcasters, a freedom essential to navig..."

### A Singular Setting Refracted Through Multiple Lenses

The structural genius of *Dekalog* lies in its setting: a singular, brutalist Warsaw tower block complex. This shared location serves as a microcosm of Polish society, where characters' lives occasionally intersect, creating a tapestry of interconnected narratives. Although originally envisioned as a project for ten different directors, Kieślowski's deepening attachment to the material led him to direct all ten hour-long episodes himself. However, he retained a core conceptual idea: employing a different cinematographer for almost every episode. This stylistic choice proved masterful. The drab concrete of the tower block is constantly reimagined, refracted through distinct visual palettes and compositional styles that reflect the varied emotional and moral tones of each specific story.

> "Decalogue in its final form consists of 10hour long episodes stylized with Roman numerals each of which is nominally related to one of the biblical commandments. ... All 10 episodes are set in the same Warsaw Tower Block complex with an overlapping ensemble cast. The 10 episodes had originally been intended for 10 different directors, but Koshlovski found himself unable to relinquish control over the project. He did however make sure to hire a different cinematographer for each film with only Pyota Soachinsky returning for two episodes. As a result, the same setting becomes refracted through a range of visual pallets and compositional styles."

While the series is nominally anchored to the Ten Commandments, its approach to the biblical text is rarely didactic. Some episodes tackle their corresponding commandment directly—such as the injunction against killing—while others are far more oblique and challenging to decipher. This ambiguity is where the series thrives. It is born from a compelling intellectual tension between Piesiewicz's steadfast religious convictions and Kieślowski's pervasive, somewhat detached secular humanism. This dynamic ensures that *Dekalog* never preaches; instead, it interrogates. The commandments act less as rigid rules and more as philosophical sounding boards against which the messy realities of human behavior are tested.

> "In truth, it is easy to overstate the importance of Decalogue's biblical framing device. While some commandments, thou shalt not kill, thou shalt not coveret thy neighbors goods, are tackled directly, the relevance of others to the on-screen drama is far from obvious. The understated tension between Piovich's more committed religiosity and Kiosky's cool humanism keeps every scenario engaging over the 10-hour plus runtime of the full series."

This deeply humanistic approach represented a logical evolution of Kieślowski's earlier career. Before establishing himself in fiction, Kieślowski was a prolific documentarian, capturing the unvarnished reality of the Polish working class throughout the 1970s. However, the omnipresent threat of state censorship and the inherent limitations of non-fiction filmmaking in an authoritarian regime eventually disillusioned him. He began to view fiction as a more potent, elusive, and ultimately more truthful medium for dissecting society. By creating allegories and fictionalizing reality, he could convey deeper truths about the Polish experience without drawing the immediate ire of political censors. *Dekalog* represents the apotheosis of this method, merging the acute observational skills of a documentarian with the poetic freedom of a dramatist.

> "Kishlovski had initially planned to make a career in non-fiction and from 1968 until 1980 he directed more than 20 documentary shorts, TV films and features about the working lives of ordinary poles. ... The censorship that certain of these films were subjected to helped to convince Kiovski to abandon documentary for fiction as he saw it a more refined medium for truthtelling in an authoritarian state."

### Moral Ambiguity and Filming "Through Glass"

The narrative tapestry of *Dekalog* is immensely rich, deliberately resisting straightforward moralization or easy summation. Each hour-long episode presents an intricate, often devastating scenario. The very first episode sets a tragic tone, focusing on a linguistics professor whose absolute faith in scientific reason and computer programming tragically fails to protect his young son from a fatal accident on the ice. This tension between empirical logic and unpredictable fate is a recurring motif. Throughout the series, the tower block is haunted by specters of death, romantic dissolution, and profound familial crises. Kieślowski thrusts his unassuming protagonists into seemingly impossible ethical dilemmas: a violinist in *Dekalog 2* agonizes over an abortion while her husband hovers near death; *Dekalog 4* grapples with the complexities of maternity and incestual tension; and *Dekalog 8* confronts the enduring trauma of the Holocaust through the lens of an ethics professor's past choices.

> "The richness of Decalogue resists easy summary. Every viewer will have their favorites among the huge ensemble cast. The series opens with one of its most heartbreaking episodes about the linguistics professor and amateur computer programmer Kushtov whose indoctrination of his precocious son into the world of scientific reason fails to prevent bitter misfortune. ... The entanglements of romance and family tend to prompt moral reckoning in Kilovski's world."

Crucially, in navigating these dense moral minefields, Kieślowski resolutely refuses to judge his characters. He adopts an observational stance that he once described to a critic as filming "as if through glass." This metaphor operates on multiple levels. Literally, it reflects the architectural reality of the tower block, where neighbors observe each other through layers of windowpane. Metaphorically, it describes Kieślowski's directorial philosophy: a cool, empathetic detachment. He allows the intense emotional turmoil of his subjects to unfold organically, without imposing an external moral verdict. The camera remains an observer, presenting human frailty with a profound sense of melancholy and understanding rather than condemnation.

> "Throughout Decalogue, Kilovski avoids easy judgment of his characters. His 1988 statement to critic Bjenna Yanitka that all films are made as if through glass captures not just the proliferating windows of the tower block through which we glimpse the lives of its inhabitants but also his sense of cool removed from the intensity of the emotional turmoil behind each front door."

The sheer breadth of human experience contained within *Dekalog* is perhaps best exemplified by the two episodes that Kieślowski later expanded into full-length feature films. *A Short Film About Killing* (expanded from *Dekalog 5*) is a visceral, unflinching, and intensely brutal examination of murder and state-sanctioned execution, presenting both acts in unbearable detail. In stark contrast, *A Short Film About Love* (expanded from *Dekalog 6*) is a delicate, surprisingly tender exploration of infatuation and romantic cynicism. It follows a lonely young postal worker who obsessively observes an older woman in a neighboring apartment through a telescope. While the premise echoes the voyeuristic suspense of Hitchcock's *Rear Window*, Kieślowski strips away the thriller mechanics. Instead, he focuses entirely on the poignant, often devastating gap between an adolescent's idealized perception of love and the complex, messy reality of adult relationships.

> "The sheer range of human drama on display is encapsulated in the two short film expansions. A short film about killing is Kishlovski at his most brutal. ... Conversely, a short film about love is almost shockingly tender in the face of human frailty. 19-year-old Tomic is an orphaned postal worker who rents a room from his best friend's mother. Each night at 8:30, he watches through a telescope as the beautiful 30-something Magda..."

### Empathy and the Polish Society as Subject

The conclusion of the *Dekalog* narratives, particularly as seen in the expanded *A Short Film About Love*, underscores the profound humanism that defines Kieślowski's work. While the original television episode (*Dekalog 6*) concludes with a harsh, abrupt realization for the young voyeur, the feature-length expansion provides a more nuanced, resonant denouement. It shifts the perspective to Magda, the object of his affection, allowing her to experience a mirrored fascination. This shift culminates in a breathtaking moment of mutual recognition. It moves beyond the mechanics of voyeurism into a space of pure empathy—a love that, as critics have noted, demands nothing more than the privilege of sight, asking for no active reciprocation.

> "Decalogue 6 ends abruptly with Tomic renouncing his love for Magda. The expanded film version, however, allows us more time with Magda in the aftermath as she begins to mirror Tomk's former fascination with her, leading to a stunning climactic scene in which the pair encounter one another in a rarified moment of recognition. what Hines calls the love of pure empathy that asks only for the privilege of seeing its object..."

The central visual motif of the telescope in this narrative is no accident. It serves as a literal manifestation of Kieślowski’s stated philosophy of filming "through glass." The telescope isolates, magnifies, and separates; it allows observation without intervention. Yet, through this very act of detached observation, a profound, almost tragic connection is forged. This dynamic is not limited to the story of Tomek and Magda; it is the animating spirit of the entire *Dekalog* project.

> "Tomic's telescope makes literal Ki Schlovski's notion of filming quote through glass. Whilst Tomic and Magda's broader story along with all the others that make up Decalogue demonstrate that all along the objects of Kashlosky's quote pure empathy was nothing less than Polish society itself."

Ultimately, the true subject of *Dekalog* is not the Ten Commandments, nor is it merely the isolated individuals wrestling with their personal demons in a concrete tower block. The ultimate object of Kieślowski's "pure empathy" is Polish society itself. By meticulously documenting the localized, specific struggles of his countrymen during a time of immense historical transition, Kieślowski succeeded in creating a masterwork that speaks to the universal human condition. The series transcends its geographical and temporal origins, standing as a timeless exploration of what it means to live, err, and connect with others in an imperfect world.

## Conclusion & Takeaways

Krzysztof Kieślowski’s *Dekalog* remains a towering achievement in world cinema because it successfully bridges the gap between the deeply specific and the universally resonant. Born out of the political and moral anxieties of late-communist Poland, the series used the allegorical framework of the Ten Commandments not as a strict moral guide, but as a mirror reflecting the complexities, tragedies, and occasional moments of grace in everyday human existence.

The primary takeaway from analyzing *Dekalog* is the power of empathetic observation. By choosing to film "through glass," Kieślowski proved that a director does not need to overtly judge their characters or spoon-feed ideological messages to an audience. Instead, by presenting human frailty with unblinking clarity and profound empathy, he allowed viewers to project their own moral anxieties onto the screen. Furthermore, the strategic use of allegory to bypass censorship demonstrates how artistic constraints can birth astonishing creativity. Ultimately, *Dekalog* endures not because it provides answers to the moral questions it raises, but because it asks those questions with unparalleled cinematic beauty and emotional depth, proving that the deepest universal truths are often found in the most localized, unassuming settings.

In addition to its thematic resonance, *Dekalog* represents a masterclass in collaborative filmmaking. Kieślowski's decision to utilize a different cinematographer for nearly every episode ensured that the series remained visually dynamic despite its static primary location. This rotating lens approach forced a constant reevaluation of the Warsaw tower block, transforming it from a mere backdrop into a multi-faceted character in its own right. It could appear bleak and oppressive in one episode, and tinged with a melancholy beauty in the next. This visual diversity perfectly complemented the thematic diversity of the stories being told, proving that a unified vision does not require rigid stylistic uniformity. The resulting tapestry is a testament to the power of trusting one's collaborators and embracing different perspectives to illuminate a central, unifying theme. The enduring legacy of *Dekalog* is its ability to speak across generations and cultures, a localized Polish story that manages to encapsulate the entirety of the human experience.

Ultimately, Kieślowski achieved something exceedingly rare in the medium of television or cinema: he created a work that is simultaneously intellectually rigorous and deeply, agonizingly human. *Dekalog* does not offer platitudes or easy resolutions. It does not shy away from the brutality of state power or the devastating consequences of personal failure. Yet, through its unwavering gaze—that compassionate, non-judgmental observation "through glass"—it finds moments of grace and connection that make the suffering bearable. Kubrick’s assessment remains as accurate today as when it was first penned; the brilliance of *Dekalog* is that the ideas sneak up on you. You begin by watching a simple story about a boy and a computer, or a voyeur and his telescope, and only later, long after the credits have rolled, do you realize that your heart has been quietly, profoundly broken and reshaped by the experience.
