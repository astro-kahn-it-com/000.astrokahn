## Executive Summary
This video delves into the fascinating concept of narrative projection, specifically analyzing the differing approaches taken by two distinct indie games: *Blaseball* and *Where Cards Fall*. By comparing these titles, the developers and narrative designers explore how players project themselves, their emotions, and their interpretations onto the frameworks provided by the games. *Where Cards Fall* represents an authored, meditative, single-player experience with a fixed narrative where players project personal meaning onto ambiguous moments and spatial puzzles. In stark contrast, *Blaseball* is a massively multiplayer, community-driven sports simulation characterized by deep absurdity and a lack of traditional authorship, forcing a massive, communal narrative projection. The speakers discuss the vital importance of "closing doors" (making choices permanent and impactful), the delicate balance between authoring a story and pulling back to allow for player interpretation, and the evolution of narrative projection from solitary experiences to emergent, collective storytelling on a massive scale. Ultimately, this deep dive provides a profound look at how modern game design can empower players to become active co-authors of the narrative experience.

The discussion begins by establishing the core framework of narrative projection, contrasting the deeply personal, single-player experience of *Where Cards Fall* with the chaotic, community-driven nature of *Blaseball*. The speakers articulate that narrative projection isn't a singular phenomenon but rather a spectrum of how players engage with authored systems. In a heavily authored game, projection might involve finding personal resonances in abstract themes. In a systemic game, it involves constructing the narrative from emergent events. The panel explores how the specific constraints and mechanics of a game serve as the language through which these projections are fostered. They emphasize that the level of authorship—how much the developer dictates versus how much they leave open—directly influences the style and depth of player projection. This initial segment acts as a grounding thesis, defining the terms and outlining the boundaries of the discussion before diving into specific examples.

### Chapter 2: Authored Ambiguity in *Where Cards Fall*

The team describes their intentional approach to creating a meditative, reflective space that encourages projection through ambiguity. The concept of moving through distinct "memories" and the structural flow of the puzzles are framed as deliberate choices to guide the player's internal narrative journey. The discussion highlights the balance required to author a compelling world while leaving enough "negative space" for the player's personal projection to thrive.

### Chapter 3: The Importance of "Closing Doors"
A pivotal moment in the discussion centers on the narrative design principle of "closing doors"—the necessity of making player choices permanent to instill a sense of weight and consequence. The panel examines how this concept manifests differently in the two featured games. In *Where Cards Fall*, closing doors is largely metaphorical; each completed puzzle solidifies a memory, moving the player forward in a fixed narrative and prompting reflection on past, immutable choices. In *Blaseball*, however, closing doors is literal, immediate, and communal. The live-service, constantly evolving nature of the game means that when the community makes a massive choice (like opening a forbidden book), the consequences are permanent. You cannot reload a previous save. This permanence, they argue, is crucial for creating stakes and forcing players to deeply invest in the ongoing narrative projection, regardless of whether the game is a solitary reflection or a massive community event.

### Chapter 5: Synthesizing the Spectrum of Design
In the final chapter, the panel synthesizes their observations, emphasizing that neither the highly authored approach of *Where Cards Fall* nor the chaotic, emergent approach of *Blaseball* is inherently superior. Instead, they represent different points on the spectrum of narrative projection. They reiterate that even in massive multiplayer experiences, communal storytelling often mirrors the ways players have historically gathered to discuss single-player games. The core takeaway is the importance of intentionality in design: knowing exactly when to assert authorship and when to deliberately step back. ## Conclusion & Takeaways
The exploration of narrative projection through the lenses of *Where Cards Fall* and *Blaseball* reveals a nuanced landscape of modern game design. The primary takeaway is that storytelling in games is not solely the responsibility of the developer; it is a collaborative process heavily reliant on the psychological engagement of the player.

Firstly, intentional ambiguity is a powerful tool. By stripping away explicit narrative delivery systems like dialogue and cinematic cuts, developers can create spaces—often termed "negative space"—where players are compelled to insert their own emotions and experiences, leading to a deeper, more personal connection with the material.

Secondly, the concept of consequence—"closing doors"—is essential across all types of games. Whether it is the metaphorical finality of moving past a memory in a linear game or the literal permanence of a community vote in a live-service simulation, permanence creates the necessary stakes for players to care about their projections.

Finally, the phenomenon of communal narrative projection demonstrates the immense potential of emergent storytelling. When systems provide complex, overlapping, and sometimes indifferent mechanics, communities will naturally band together to synthesize meaning, create lore, and form shared identities. Understanding where a game sits on the spectrum of authorship—knowing precisely when to guide the player and when to let go—is critical to fostering rich, engaging narrative projection.
